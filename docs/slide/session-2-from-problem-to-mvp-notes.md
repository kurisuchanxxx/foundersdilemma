# Founder’s Dilemma · Session 2 · From problem to MVP

## 01 · From problem to MVP.

Second session. Goals: use customer discovery to reduce uncertainty, decide when to launch, and read the product-market fit score. Teams play rounds 2 and 3. Open with the round 1 debriefing: leaderboard and news in projector view.

## 02 · Today’s three hours.

The rhythm of every session from the second to the fifth: a debriefing of the previous round, a mini-lesson on the concept the day’s rounds will need, two rounds with the timer on the projector while the teacher circulates among the teams, and a wrap-up with the round report and the decision memo assignment.

## 03 · Get out of the building.

Steve Blank’s customer development (The Four Steps to the Epiphany, 2005; later The Startup Owner’s Manual with Bob Dorf, 2012) puts the search for customers alongside product development. The four steps: customer discovery (find out whether the problem exists and who has it), customer validation (show that someone buys and that the sale repeats), customer creation (create demand at scale), company building (move from an organization that searches to a company that executes). The first two are the “search,” the last two the “execution”; if validation fails, you go back to discovery. Blank’s motto is “there are no facts inside the building”: inside the building there are only opinions. Question for the class: how many conversations with real customers did you have before choosing your idea?

## 04 · A business plan is a list of hypotheses.

Eric Ries (The Lean Startup, 2011) calls the hypotheses the whole plan rests on “leap-of-faith assumptions”: the value hypothesis (the product creates value for the people who use it) and the growth hypothesis (how new customers will discover it). The practical point is that a business plan is not something to execute but something to test, starting from the riskiest hypothesis. Tests count when they measure behavior (sign-ups, purchases, return visits) rather than opinions (“yes, I’d use it”). Question for the class: what is your company’s riskiest hypothesis, and which of the two kinds is it?

## 05 · Build, measure, learn. As fast as you can.

Eric Ries’s build-measure-learn loop: a startup’s unit of progress is not code written but validated learning, meaning what customer data confirms or disproves. What matters is how fast you go around the loop, not how perfect each lap is. So an MVP is not “a small product” but an experiment: it has to be enough to learn from, not enough to please. Ries also warns against “vanity metrics,” the numbers that always go up (total users, visits) and don’t tell you whether the hypothesis holds. Question for the class: which number would tell you that you are wrong?

## 06 · An MVP doesn’t have to be a product.

Three kinds of MVP that Ries describes in The Lean Startup. Dropbox: reliable file syncing was hard to build, so Drew Houston first showed in a video how it would work; by Houston’s account the beta waitlist went from about 5,000 to 75,000 sign-ups overnight (sources date the video anywhere between 2007 and 2008). Zappos: in 1999 Swinmurn tests whether people will buy shoes online without any inventory, photographing pairs in stores and buying them at full price for every order, often at a loss. It is a “Wizard of Oz” MVP: the customer sees an automated service, and behind it there is a person. Food on the Table (Austin): a “concierge” MVP, where the founders personally serve the first customer and add customers and automation only once they understand what works. Question for the class: which of these three MVPs could you run yourselves, this week, for less than €500?

## 07 · fit

The definition comes from Marc Andreessen, in the post The only thing that matters (June 2007, part of the PMarca Guide to Startups series), where he ties it to the Andy Rachleff line seen in session 1. Andreessen adds that you can always feel whether fit is happening: when it isn’t, customers don’t quite get the value, word of mouth isn’t spreading, usage isn’t growing that fast, reviews are lukewarm, and sales cycles take too long; when it is, customers buy the product just as fast as you can make it, and you hire people just to keep up with demand. For Andreessen, a startup’s life divides into before and after product-market fit. Question for the class: what signals would you see in your numbers if fit arrived?

## 08 · One question to measure fit.

The test comes from Sean Ellis, who led early marketing and growth at Dropbox, LogMeIn and Eventbrite: you ask active users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product. Ellis observed that companies that went on to grow well were above 40% “very disappointed”; it is a leading indicator, not final proof. Rahul Vohra, founder of Superhuman (an email app), told the story on First Round Review and on the company blog: he started at 22% and brought the figure to 58% in about a year. He segmented his users, worked out what the most enthusiastic ones loved and what was holding back the undecided ones, and gave half of the roadmap to each of the two. Question for the class: who would you ask, all users or only active ones, and why?

## 09 · Change the strategy, keep the vision.

For Eric Ries a pivot is a structured course correction, designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, the strategy or the engine of growth. It is not starting over: you keep what you have learned. YouTube (2005): according to co-founder Steve Chen, it was meant as a video dating site; nobody uploaded videos, so they opened it to everyone. Twitter (2006): it began as an internal project at Odeo, a podcasting startup put in trouble when Apple added podcasts to iTunes. Instagram (2010): Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger stripped down Burbn, a check-in app, keeping only the feature users actually used, photos. Question for the class: what signal in the data would make you decide to pivot, and what would tell you to persevere?

## 10 · Technical debt: speed today, interest tomorrow.

The metaphor comes from Ward Cunningham, in a short experience report on the WyCash software presented at the 1992 OOPSLA conference. Technical debt is not code written badly out of laziness: it is the deliberate choice to ship sooner with a temporary solution. Like a loan, it makes sense if you pay it back quickly; if you don’t, every minute spent on not-quite-right code counts as interest, paid again with every new feature (see also Martin Fowler, Technical Debt). For an MVP some debt is normal; the trouble starts when the company grows on top of that code. Question for the class: at what stage of a startup does it make sense to take on debt, and when do you have to pay it back?

## 11 · Before you build, five questions.

The five questions sum up the lesson and apply to any startup: the riskiest hypothesis (Ries), the cheapest experiment (the MVP), a metric chosen before looking at the data (so you don’t read only what confirms your view), direct contact with customers (Blank), and the choice between pivoting and persevering. Ask each team to answer in writing before round 2: the answers become the Hypotheses section of the decision memo.

## 12 · Today’s decisions.

The first dilemma usually shows up in rounds 2 and 3: ask whoever faced it to tell the class about it in the debriefing. The niche (beachhead) reaches only 30% of the industry but speeds up PMF learning; before launch, choices about customers and pricing can be changed for free, after launch they cost customers. Show the Product & PMF view: it is where teams read their score.

## 13 · Lean versus blitz: who reaches fit.

Data from the reference balancing run documented in the simulation model: 60 games with 6 bot companies each. Lean bots do deep discovery, build for quality and spend little; blitz bots launch early and spend heavily from the start. The blitz bots raise more and grow fast at first, but they never reach PMF: when the market reaches the early majority they fall into the chasm, get diluted more, and leave their founders far less wealth. Ask the teams why before you give the explanation.
