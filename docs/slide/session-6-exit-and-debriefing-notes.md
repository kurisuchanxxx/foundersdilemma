## 03 · The exit is the moment shares turn into money.

For founders, employees and investors, shares are wealth only on paper until there is an exit. The main forms: the initial public offering (IPO), which keeps the company independent and makes its shares tradable on the market; an acquisition by a strategic buyer, who pays more when it sees synergies with its own business; an acquisition by a financial buyer, typically a private equity fund, which looks at cash flows; and the acqui-hire, where the buyer is mostly after the team. There are also secondary sales, in which a shareholder sells their shares to another investor without the company changing hands. PitchBook-NVCA data have shown for years that acquisitions far outnumber IPOs by count, while IPOs carry a lot of weight by value. Question for the class: which form of exit would you prefer for your company, and who would be its natural buyer?

## 04 · Two sales, and one offer turned down.

Instagram: announced on April 9, 2012, for about $1 billion in cash and Facebook stock; according to TechCrunch the company had 13 employees, and the app had launched in October 2010. WhatsApp: a February 2014 deal worth about $16 billion, $4 billion in cash and $12 billion in stock, plus $3 billion in restricted stock units for the founders and employees, vesting over four years: an example of how a buyer uses vesting to keep the team. Facebook: in 2006 Yahoo offered $1 billion; Zuckerberg turned it down, convinced the company could grow much bigger. Sources: Facebook’s announcement (April 2012) and press coverage at the time; the SEC filing for the WhatsApp deal (February 2014); accounts by Peter Thiel and in the Facebook biographies. Question for the class: hindsight makes it easy; with what information would you have decided, back then, to sell or to say no?

## 05 · The sale price is not what the founders take home.

The waterfall is the order in which the sale price flows down to the shareholders. With a 1x non-participating preference, the investor takes the better of two options: getting back what it invested, or converting to common stock and taking its share. In the example (numbers made up for the lesson), at €12M the 40% would be worth €4.8M, less than the €8M invested, so the investor takes the preference and the founders are left with €4M: a third of the price, despite owning 60% of the shares. At €40M converting pays more, and the split goes back to pro rata. Preferences matter a lot in low-priced sales and barely at all in high-priced ones. This builds on the session 5 lesson on term sheet clauses. Question for the class: on your cap table, below what price would the founders get nothing?

## 06 · Five questions to ask about an offer.

Selling is a choice between a certain price today and an uncertain value tomorrow. The first question is the heart of the Facebook and Yahoo case: saying no makes sense only if you believe, with good reasons, that the company will be worth much more and that you will actually get there. The second points back to the waterfall: the same price can be great for the investors and disappointing for the founders. The third is a reminder that if the founders have lost control of the board, the decision may not be theirs to make. The fourth is about people: employees with options, customers, a product the buyer might shut down. The fifth is about context: acquisition prices rise and fall with the market. Question for the class: if you got an offer today, which of these questions would make you say no?

## 07 · Tell the story of what happened, not of what you hoped for.

Many founders write a post-mortem when their company shuts down: these are exactly the texts CB Insights drew on for the analysis of why startups fail that you saw in session 1. A good post-mortem separates facts from interpretations, reconstructs each decision with the information available at the time (not with what you know today), and tells apart what was up to the team from what was not. Annie Duke (Thinking in Bets, 2018) calls resulting the tendency to judge a decision by its result: a reasonable choice can end badly through bad luck, and a reckless one can turn out well. In the final report, the Three key decisions section asks for exactly this: context, alternatives, choice, outcome and what you would do again. Question for the class: which of your decisions went well by luck?

## 08 · Five ideas to take with you.

One sentence for each session of the module, from founding to exit: the startup as a search for a model and the weight of the market (session 1), product-market fit (session 2), unit economics (session 3), the cost of capital in equity and control and Wasserman’s dilemma (session 5), the exit waterfall (today). People (session 4) run through all five. Ask each team which of the five it learned the hard way: it is a good way into the presentations.

## 09 · Sell, and at what price?

An offer at 1.5 times the valuation does not mean 1.5 times for the founders: preferences are paid first, then the rest goes to the shareholders pro rata, and once you sell you are no longer King. Have teams look at their cap table before they decide. In acquisitions between teams, the buyer pays up front, takes over cash, debt and prepaid contracts, gets all the people and, if the company is in the same industry, 85% of the customers. If the buyer no longer has the cash when the offer is accepted, the offer lapses.

## 10 · Closing the game.

The standings follow the criterion chosen at the start (recommended: founders’ wealth). The in-game result is only 10% of the grade and is scored in bands, so that luck is not rewarded: a failure with a convincing post-mortem is worth 6 points out of 10. Each team downloads its own Team report: a printable page with numbers, charts, round-by-round decisions, funding, dilemmas and post-mortem questions. ## 11 · Eight minutes to tell a company’s story.

An excellent presentation has a clear thread and a thesis about what worked and what did not, chooses its two or three charts or numbers well, connects the lessons to theory and acknowledges the limits when answering questions. Keep time: exactly 8 minutes, then 4 for questions from the class and the teacher.

## 12 · Four questions about the whole journey.

For the first question, ask teams to answer with the waterfall in hand: which price leaves the founders an amount that makes selling worthwhile? For the second, the guide suggests having teams tell the story of the negotiation: who proposed, who accepted and why, and whether the price was fair given the sellers’ waterfall and the buyer’s cash. The fourth question is a great prompt for assessing critical thinking: the next slide lists the simplifications the model declares.

## 13 · Where the game simplifies.

Show this slide only after the class has answered the fourth question: it lets you compare their observations with the simplifications the model declares. Two more to mention if nobody spots them: the valuation counts half of net cash, and loans are not repaid at the exit; investors decide by transparent rules plus a little noise, with no open negotiation. Discussing them is part of the learning, and the final report has a section just for this, the reality check.

## 14 · The last assignment.

The Team report already contains numbers, charts and round-by-round decisions, so the final report can focus on analysis. In the Funding and control section, teams place themselves on the Rich or King chart and explain why; in the Reality check they pick a real case similar to their own trajectory and say where the game simplifies. There are five Lessons learned, each transferable to a real project. The rubric weighs data analysis and use of theory (25% each), quality of the post-mortem (20%), finance and control (15%) and clarity (15%).
