# Founder’s Dilemma · Session 4 · People and organization

Teacher notes, slide by slide. The slides are in the PDF with the same name.

## 01 · People and organization.

Fourth session. Goals: plan the first hires, use equity as compensation, and manage morale, hours and founder stress. Teams play rounds 6 and 7, usually when the market is reaching the early majority, so keep an eye on the chasm today too.

## 02 · Today’s three hours.

Today’s lesson is shorter (35 minutes). That leaves more time for the rounds, where the hiring auctions need discussion inside each team.

## 03 · The first people make the company.

In the early years every person carries enormous weight: they bring skills, but also habits that become the company’s culture. In the search phase you need generalists who can change roles; specialists become valuable once the model is found and has to scale. A bad hire does not cost just the salary: it costs the founders’ time to onboard the person and later to manage the exit, and it weighs on everyone else’s morale. The line comes from Ben Horowitz, The Hard Thing About Hard Things (2014), who credits it to advice from Colin Powell: better someone with an exceptional strength you need right now, flaws and all, than someone with no flaws and no strengths. Question for the class: which role would you hire first in your company, and why that one?

## 04 · Employee, contractor or advisor.

Not every skill needs a hire. An employee brings continuity and knowledge that stays, but costs salary, equity and onboarding time, and a mistake is slow to fix. A contractor gives you capacity right away on a specific job, but what they learn leaves with them, and someone outside the company has fewer reasons to care about long-term quality. An advisor brings contacts and credibility in exchange for a few hours and a small stake, usually with a shorter vesting schedule: it only works if the help is concrete and verifiable. Question for the class: which of the three would you choose for your next need, and what would you give up?

## 05 · Employee equity: less salary, more future.

A startup cannot pay what a large company pays, so it offers a share of the future: stock options give the right to buy shares at a price fixed on the grant date, and they are worth something only if the company grows. Options come from a reserved option pool and vest over time, usually 4 years with a 1-year cliff. The higher the risk, the more equity it takes to convince a strong person to leave a safe job. According to Carta data, the median pool is around 12% after the seed and grows with every round, to almost 19% at Series D; investors often ask for it to be expanded before they come in, so the dilution falls on the existing shareholders (we will see this in session 5). Question for the class: would you accept a 20% lower salary in exchange for 0.5% of the company? What does it depend on?

## 06 · “Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.”

Brooks’s law comes from Fred Brooks’s experience managing IBM’s OS/360 operating system: adding manpower to a late software project makes it later, because newcomers have to be trained by the people already doing the work, and the coordination effort grows faster than the team. With n people, the pairs who need to talk to each other number n × (n - 1) ÷ 2. For a startup the lesson is that growing headcount is not free: it takes structure, clear ownership and small groups. Question for the class: at what headcount would your team need a first layer of managers?

## 07 · cultura

Culture is not what is written on the walls, but what the company rewards and what it tolerates. The line comes from the Netflix Culture: Freedom & Responsibility deck, published in 2009 by Reed Hastings with Patty McCord, then head of people: it became one of the most widely read documents in Silicon Valley, and Sheryl Sandberg called it perhaps the most important document ever to come out of the Valley. In the same deck Netflix points out that Enron also had beautiful values displayed in its lobby. In a startup, culture forms with the first people and the first hard decisions: who gets hired, who gets promoted, what gets forgiven. Question for the class: what behavior would your team never tolerate?

## 08 · More hours do not mean more work.

John Pencavel (Stanford, IZA Discussion Paper 8129, 2014, later published in the Economic Journal) analyzed data on workers, mostly women, in British munitions factories during World War I. Up to about 49 hours a week, output grew in proportion to hours; beyond that it grew less and less, and at 70 hours it was close to what 56 hours produced. Missing a weekly day of rest also hurt: six eight-hour days produced a little more than 70 hours spread over seven days. It is an observational study of manual work, so treat it as an indication, but the lesson for founders holds: past a certain point, extra hours are paid for in mistakes and fatigue. Question for the class: how many hours a week do you think you could sustain for a whole year, not just a month?

## 09 · The founder is the scarcest resource.

Michael Freeman and colleagues (The prevalence and co-occurrence of psychiatric conditions among entrepreneurs and their families, Small Business Economics, 2019) compared 242 entrepreneurs with a group of non-entrepreneurs: the entrepreneurs more often reported a history of depression (30%), ADHD (29%), substance use (12%) and bipolar disorder (11%). The data come from a questionnaire, not from diagnoses, but the message is clear: founder health is a company resource, not a private matter. The four practices are common-sense guidance, not findings of the study. Question for the class: who on your team would be the first to notice that a founder is at the limit?

## 10 · Before you hire, five questions.

The five questions sum up the lesson and apply to any real startup: the first two are about whom to hire and in what form, the third about the price in equity, the fourth about coordination as the team grows, the last about whether the founders’ workload is sustainable. Ask the teams to answer them before making offers to candidates in today’s round, and to bring the answers into the decision memo.

## 11 · Today’s decisions.

In the auction, all offers for the same candidate, including those from different teams, are compared: the best score above the threshold wins. An offer that comes close to the threshold gets a counter-offer (higher salary and more equity) that stays valid for the next round. The score weighs salary and equity against the candidate’s expectations, fit with the risk of the company’s stage, talent reputation and interest in the sector, with penalties for a short runway, low morale and stressed founders. The founder network allows up to two actions toward other teams: everything is public and ends up in the news. People with unvested equity are more willing to stay.

## 12 · Three questions for rounds 6 and 7.

On the second question: candidates who lean toward equity give more weight to the stake, and the equity they ask for changes with the company’s stage. On the third: an offer lost close to the threshold leaves a counter-offer valid for the next round, so the game is not always over. Connect the answers to morale and runway: a runway that is too short penalizes every offer.
