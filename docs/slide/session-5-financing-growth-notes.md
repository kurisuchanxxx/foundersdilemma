# Founder’s Dilemma · Session 5 · Financing growth

Teacher notes, slide by slide. The slides are in the PDF with the same name.

## 01 · Financing growth.

Fifth session, and the heaviest on theory: the lesson runs 45 minutes and there is a 20-minute term sheet activity. Goals: compare sources of funding, read a term sheet, understand dilution, the option pool shuffle and liquidation preferences, and face the dilemma of wealth versus control. The class plays rounds 8 and 9. In the simulations, failures cluster in rounds 7-10, so runway is a topic today too.

## 02 · Today’s three hours.

Time is tight. If the debrief of rounds 6 and 7 runs long, shorten the rounds rather than the term sheet activity: that is the moment dilution becomes concrete. In a graduate finance course (Advanced complexity), give this session more time: the Funding page includes the exit waterfall simulator.

## 03 · Every euro you raise has a price, even when you can’t see it.

The sources roughly follow the life of the company: first the founders’ own money and customers’ money, then friends and family, grants and subsidized loans, business angels, and finally venture capital (seed, Series A and later rounds). The point of the lesson is that no source is free: debt has to be repaid, grants take time and come with strings, equity costs ownership and often control, and venture capital comes with an expectation of very fast growth. Question for the class: for your startup, which price are you most willing to pay, and which one not at all?

## 04 · Mailchimp: twenty years without venture capital.

Mailchimp, the email marketing service founded in 2001 in Atlanta, grew for twenty years on revenue alone, with no outside investors. In September 2021 Intuit announced it would acquire the company for about $12 billion in cash and stock; the deal closed on November 1, 2021 (sources: TechCrunch, September 13, 2021; Axios). The founders kept control and almost all of the ownership until the sale. Question for the class: what made it possible to grow without investors, and in which industries would it have been impossible?

## 05 · A few big wins pay for everything else.

The Correlation Ventures data (more than 21,000 financings between 2004 and 2013, reported by Seth Levine in 2014) show how returns are distributed: about two financings in three return less than was invested, only 10% return 5x or more, and only 4% return 10x or more. Note that these are financings, not companies. Peter Thiel draws the rule from this: in a power-law portfolio only the exceptional case matters, so every investment has to be able to return the entire fund on its own. The consequence for founders: a VC prefers a small chance of a huge outcome over a high chance of a good one. Question for the class: can your startup become big enough to interest a VC? If not, what changes?

## 06 · SAFE or priced round.

The SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) was introduced by Y Combinator at the end of 2013 to make early investments simpler and faster: no interest and no maturity date, just the right to receive shares at the first priced round. In 2018 YC published the post-money version, where the cap is measured after all the SAFEs, so each investor knows right away what stake they will get and the founders see the real dilution. The typical risk is piling up SAFEs without doing the math: at the first priced round they all convert at once. In the game, a new SAFE is rejected if it would push outstanding SAFEs above 60% of the company. Question for the class: why would an investor accept not knowing today what stake they will end up with?

## 07 · Dilution in two lines, and in three rounds.

Two formulas are enough to read any round: post-money is pre-money plus the investment, and the new investor’s stake is the investment divided by the post-money. All existing shareholders are diluted in the same proportion. Dilution compounds: three rounds that each sell 20% leave the founders 0.8 cubed, about 51%, before you even count the option pool. Dilution is not bad in itself: what matters is what your stake is worth, not how big it is. In the game, a dilution above 40% in a single round is always rejected. Question for the class: would you rather own 100% of €1 million or 20% of €50 million?

## 08 · The clauses that move value.

Brad Feld and Jason Mendelson (Venture Deals) split term sheet clauses into two families: economics (valuation, option pool, preferences) and control (board seats, veto rights). In the example, at €12M the 25% would be worth €3M, less than the €5M preference: the investor takes back the €5M and the other shareholders are left with €7M. At €40M the 25% is worth €10M, so converting pays off. The option pool shuffle moves value less visibly: if the pool is created before the round, the founders’ “real” pre-money is lower than the one on paper. Question for the class: at what exit value do the founders in this example start to make anything?

## 09 · Rich or king?

This is the dilemma the game is named after (Noam Wasserman, “The Founder’s Dilemma,” Harvard Business Review, February 2008). Every funding round trades equity, and often board seats, for capital, people and connections: you grow more, but you decide less. Wasserman does not say that either choice is right: he says founders need to know which one they want, because financing decisions push them toward one or the other. Question for the class: how much of the company do you want to own at the end, and how much do you care about staying CEO?

## 10 · The board picks the CEO, even when the CEO is the founder.

The figures come from the same Wasserman article (Harvard Business Review, February 2008), based on 212 American startups: three years after founding, half of the founders were no longer CEO, and fewer than a quarter still led the company at its IPO. The replacement often comes right after a success, when the company needs different skills and the board, where investors carry more and more weight, makes the call. Jobs was pushed aside in 1985 and returned to Apple in 1997; Dorsey gave up the Twitter CEO role in October 2008 and took it back in 2015. Question for the class: at what point should the founder be the one to propose a new CEO?

## 11 · Before you raise, five questions.

The five questions sum up the lesson and apply to any real fundraise. Use them before the pitches in rounds 8 and 9: each team answers in writing, then decides whether to raise and from whom. The second question is the most practical: a round should buy enough runway to reach a proof point that pushes the valuation up (a launch, product-market fit, a revenue threshold).

## 12 · Today’s decisions.

All of the round’s pitches, from every team and every bot, are evaluated together: each investor signs the best ones by interest until it runs out of slots and capital; the others get a rejection that names who got the money. A deal closes with an interest of at least 0.55 and an ask within 115% of the fair valuation; between 0.4 and 0.55 an alternative term sheet comes back. The rescue round covers the deficit plus 3 months of burn and adds a board seat; how many rescues are possible depends on the difficulty (3 in Forgiving, 1 in Standard, none in Realistic). An acquisition offer must be worth at least half of the other company’s valuation and cannot exceed the buyer’s cash.

## 13 · A term sheet on the whiteboard.

The formula holds for a round with no other changes: the founders’ stake shrinks by the ratio of pre-money to post-money. If the class’s result is higher than the cap table’s, the usual culprits are the option pool shuffle (the pool grows before the round and dilutes only the existing shareholders) or the conversion of SAFEs. Close by asking: how much of the valuation would really reach the founders, with the current preferences? It is also the third debriefing question.

## 14 · Four questions for rounds 8 and 9.

On the fourth question: investor interest rises with PMF, growth, reputation, team strength, the founders’ fundraising skill and the market climate, and drops if the valuation asked is above the fair one, if the amount is outside the investor’s check size, or if runway is under 3 months. In the game, the rejection names who got the money: start from there. In the simulations, teams with a high burn suffer most when everyone is asking for money at the same time.

## 15 · Before the next session.

Remind the teams that the next session includes the presentations: 8 minutes each plus 4 for questions, covering their strategy, three key decisions, what they would do differently and the link to a real case. It pays to prepare the slides in advance, starting from the memos they have already written.
