# Founder’s Dilemma · Session 3 · Go-to-market and unit economics

Teacher notes, slide by slide. The slides are in the PDF with the same name.

## 01 · Go-to-market and unit economics.

Third session. Goals: set the price against willingness to pay, choose the channel, read CAC, LTV, churn and payback, and recognize the chasm. Teams play rounds 4 and 5: the end of the first year and the first half of the second. The mini-lesson is the longest in the module (45 minutes), and there is a short 15-minute activity on payback.

## 02 · Today’s three hours.

The usual rhythm, plus the short payback activity (15 minutes), done after the rounds with the values from the Market page. The chasm usually arrives around the middle of the game: the mentor announces it one round ahead, and that is the right moment to discuss it.

## 03 · Decide the price before the product.

Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke, consultants at Simon-Kucher, argue that many innovations fail not because the product is bad but because nobody checked in time how much customers were willing to pay: the book’s subtitle is “How Smart Companies Design the Product Around the Price.” The 72% is the authors’ own estimate, based on their consulting experience, not an independent study: present it as such. Question for the class: at what point in your project did you ask a customer how much they would pay?

## 04 · Three ways to set a price.

The three classic approaches to pricing. Cost-based pricing guarantees a margin but leaves money on the table when perceived value is high, and does not hold up when it is low. Competitor-based pricing is a good starting point but leads companies to compete on price alone. Value-based pricing starts from how much the customer saves or earns with the product, and it is the approach Ramanujam and Tacke recommend. Quick exercise: each team estimates the monthly value of its product for a typical customer, then compares it with the price it chose.

## 05 · How to get paid.

Each model moves the numbers. Subscription makes revenue predictable and turns churn into the main problem. Freemium uses the free product as an acquisition channel: it only works if free users are cheap to serve and some of them convert to paid. For self-serve products a conversion rate of 3-5% is considered good and 6-8% great (Lenny Rachitsky, analysis of more than a thousand products; a fifth of products stay below 2.5%). Usage-based pricing ties cost to the value received and grows with the customer, but makes revenue less predictable. Question for the class: which model best tracks the value your customer receives?

## 06 · Three numbers that tell you if a customer pays off.

Unit economics looks at one customer at a time: if each customer costs more than it brings in, growing loses more money, not less. The two thresholds come from David Skok (Matrix Partners, SaaS Metrics 2.0): they are rules of thumb for subscription software, not laws. An LTV/CAC far above 3 can mean the company is underinvesting in growth; a long payback requires a lot of cash even when LTV is good. Watch the CAC: it has to include every acquisition cost, salespeople’s salaries included. The next slide runs the numbers on an example.

## 07 · Software at €50 a month, number by number.

An example with made-up numbers, to redo on the board. With 4% monthly churn a customer stays 25 months on average; each month they leave €40 of margin (€50 × 80%), so LTV is €1,000 and the €300 CAC is recovered in 7.5 months. Then change one number at a time and ask what happens: with 8% churn, lifetime drops to 12.5 months, LTV to €500 and the LTV/CAC ratio to 1.7, below the threshold. Churn is often the most powerful lever.

## 08 · A market does not adopt all at once.

Everett Rogers’ curve splits the adopters of an innovation into five groups, with the classic shares of 2.5%, 13.5%, 34%, 34% and 16%. Each group buys for different reasons: innovators out of curiosity, early adopters to gain an edge before everyone else, the early majority once the solution is proven and others like them are using it. For a startup the consequence is practical: the first customers are not a sample of the market, and what convinces them is not enough to convince the ones who come next. The line between early adopters and the majority is the chasm on the next slide.

## 09 · Between visionaries and pragmatists lies a chasm.

Geoffrey Moore observes that many technology products sell well to early adopters and then stall: the pragmatists in the majority do not trust visionaries and want to see the solution working at companies like their own. To cross the chasm, Moore uses the analogy of the D-Day landing in Normandy: concentrate all your forces on one narrow segment (the beachhead), win it completely with a whole product, and from there expand into the neighboring segments. Question for the class: what is your Normandy, the smallest segment you can win completely?

## 10 · Facebook: one campus at a time.

Facebook did not launch for everyone: thefacebook.com opened on February 4, 2004 for Harvard students only, expanded in March to Stanford, Columbia and Yale and then to other universities, reached high schools in September 2005, and in September 2006 opened to anyone aged 13 or older with an email address. Each campus was a small, dense segment where the product became indispensable because all your classmates were on it: Moore’s beachhead in practice. Question for the class: what would have been the risk of opening to everyone on day one?

## 11 · Nineteen channels. You need one that works.

Gabriel Weinberg (founder of DuckDuckGo) and Justin Mares argue that many startups fail not because of the product but for lack of customers, and they propose the Bullseye framework: consider all 19 traction channels (including SEO, content marketing, online ads, PR, events, sales, business development and viral marketing), rank them from possible to most promising, test a few with cheap experiments, and focus on the one that works. The cycle repeats at every stage of growth, because the channel that works with the first customers is rarely enough later on. They also recommend splitting your time evenly between product and traction. Question for the class: which channel did you choose, and what experiment would tell you whether it works?

## 12 · Five questions before you spend on marketing.

The five questions sum up the lesson and apply to any real startup: value, pricing model, unit economics, adoption and channel. Use them as a guide for the decisions in rounds 4 and 5: each team answers in two lines before choosing price and channel, and reports the numbers in the decision memo.

## 13 · Today’s decisions.

Each channel has its own effect on CAC, churn and brand. Each company can win at most 4% of the remaining market per month, and teams in the same sector share the same market; competition raises CAC by 6% for every other team launched in the sector. From round 2, outside competitors make at most one move per round per sector, and each move becomes a news item. In the Deals tab, teams can propose partnerships to each other: this sets up the last debriefing question.

## 14 · Work out the payback by hand.

Each team does the calculation with its own values from the Market page and compares it with the payback the game shows. If the result is different, ask why: it is usually the gross margin, which in the game is the margin actually achieved in the round. Then ask which team has the shortest payback and what drives it (price, margin or CAC).

## 15 · Five questions for rounds 4 and 5.

An example of linking back to theory, from the teacher’s guide: your CAC went up because the market moved past the early adopters and your PMF was below 55; that is the chasm. On coopetition (Brandenburger and Nalebuff): in the game a partnership lasts 3 rounds; between teams in the same sector it lowers CAC and churn (alliance), between different sectors it lowers CAC and boosts word of mouth (co-marketing).
