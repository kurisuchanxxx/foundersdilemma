# Founder’s Dilemma · Session 1 · The founding team and the idea

Teacher notes, slide by slide. The slides are in the PDF with the same name.

## 01 · The founding team and the idea.

First session of the module. Goals: understand the startup as a temporary organization searching for a repeatable business model; choose the sector, the co-founders and the equity split with care. The six dots at the bottom track the module: one lights up at each session. Before class: create the class game (Classroom mode if every team has a laptop, Hot-seat with a single computer), 10 rounds with the “4 quarters, then semesters” schedule, Standard complexity, and write down the seed.

## 02 · Today’s three hours.

The practice game is only for learning the interface: each student plays 3-4 rounds alone against the bots, ungraded. Then form the teams (about 15 minutes) and have them found their companies (about 30 minutes): name, sector, founder archetypes, equity split, vesting. In Classroom mode it works well to have each team dictate its choices while you enter them, or to collect them beforehand with a form. Close with round 1 and the debriefing.

## 03 · definizione

Two definitions that complete each other. Blank stresses the search: a startup does not yet know who the customer is, what they will pay or how to reach them, and its job is to find out. Ries stresses uncertainty: everything a startup does is an experiment. Ask the class which words in the first definition matter most: “temporary” (once the model is found, the startup becomes a company), “repeatable” (it works more than once), “scalable” (it grows without costs growing at the same pace).

## 04 · A startup is not a small business.

A pizzeria, a consulting firm or a shop are businesses that execute a known model: you know who the customers are and what they pay. A startup does not. Paul Graham (Startup = Growth, 2012) adds growth: a startup is a company designed to grow fast, and at Y Combinator a good early growth rate is 5-7% a week. That is why startups are funded with risk capital: investors accept that many will fail in exchange for the few that grow enormously. Ask the class for one example per column.

## 05 · Why startups fail.

CB Insights read post-mortems written by the founders themselves: the top cause is not a lack of money but having built something nobody wanted. Running out of cash is often the consequence, not the cause. For a sense of scale: according to research by Shikhar Ghosh (Harvard Business School, 2012), about three in four venture-backed startups do not return investors’ capital. Question for the class: which of these causes can you test before spending much?

## 06 · mercato

Andy Rachleff, co-founder of Benchmark Capital, in the version Marc Andreessen made famous: of a startup’s three ingredients (team, product, market), the market matters most. A market that pulls will lift even a mediocre product; a market that is not there will sink even the best team. Question for the class: think of a well-made product that did not succeed. Was it the team or the market?

## 07 · Look for problems, not ideas.

Paul Graham, founder of Y Combinator: “The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It’s to look for problems, preferably problems you have yourself.” Ideas dreamed up at a desk tend to sound plausible and interest no one. In all three cases the problem belonged to the founders themselves, and in Slack’s case the solution came out of a failed project. Question for the class: what problem did you run into this week that nobody solves well?

## 08 · Three choices to make before anything else.

Wasserman’s “three Rs” (Relationships, Roles, Rewards) are the decisions founders make first, often in a hurry and without discussing them, and that later turn into the causes of conflict. The point of the lesson is that there is no choice that is right in absolute terms, but each one has costs worth knowing in advance. The next three slides take them one at a time.

## 09 · Who to found with.

Complementary skills reduce risk: the person who builds the product and the person who finds the customers face two different uncertainties. Wasserman observes that teams formed from friendships or family ties are comfortable at first but more fragile when the hard decisions arrive (roles, equity, departures). Ron Wayne’s story shows the other side: not every co-founder has the same tolerance for risk, and it is better to find out right away. Question for the class: on your team, who builds, who sells, who decides?

## 10 · An equal split is easy. It is not always fair.

The equity split is the decision founders make fastest and the one they renegotiate most often. Thomas Hellmann and Noam Wasserman (The First Deal: The Division of Founder Equity in New Ventures, Management Science, 2016) find that about a third of teams split equally, and that equal splits decided right away, on a handshake, are associated with lower valuations in the first round: investors read them as a sign of difficult conversations avoided. Quick exercise: each team writes down its split and one sentence that justifies it.

## 11 · Equity is earned over time: 48 months, 12-month cliff.

Vesting makes equity vest over time: the most common schedule is 48 months with a 12-month cliff, meaning nothing in the first year, a quarter at the one-year mark and then one forty-eighth a month. It protects those who stay from someone who leaves early with a big slice of the company, and investors almost always ask for it. Snapchat is the opposite example: with no written agreements, a founder’s exit ended up in court and was settled for $157.5 million. Question for the class: if one of you left in six months, what should they keep?

## 12 · Before you found, five questions.

The five questions sum up the lesson and apply to any real startup. Use them as a guide for founding: each team answers in writing before entering its choices in the game, and keeps the answers for the first decision memo. The first two come back to the market and to failure; the last three to Wasserman’s founding choices.

## 13 · Today you found the company.

The first solo game starts a two-minute guided tour of the main screens. In round 1 the teams set their first budget (R&D, marketing, operations), the customer discovery level and the development approach for the MVP. The what-if projection on the right of the screen shows the expected cash and runway at the end of the round: it is the first number to look at. Run the round timer from the instructor console.

## 14 · Three questions to close.

The same questions appear under Discussion prompts in the instructor console. A three-step structure that works: what happened (put the leaderboard and the news on screen from the projector view, and have two teams with opposite choices speak), why (link the results to a concept and open the matching card in Learn), and in the real world (a real case or a question). Reward well-argued decisions even when the outcome was unlucky: the simulation contains uncertainty on purpose.

## 15 · The decision memo: one page per round.

The decision memo template is in the Assessment and rubrics guide. Memos count for 30% of the grade and the best 6 out of 10 are graded, so one weak memo does not weigh too much. Rubric from 0 to 10: reading the data, hypotheses and decisions (weight 1.5), learning (weight 0.5). Ask for testable hypotheses, for example: with deep customer discovery, the PMF score passes 40 within two rounds.
