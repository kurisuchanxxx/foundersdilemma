# Session plan

Reference format: **6 sessions of 3 hours**, game in **Classroom (files)** or **Hot-seat** mode, **Standard** complexity, **4 quarters, then semesters** timeline, 10 rounds (about four years in the life of the company). Every session follows the same rhythm:

| Block | Time | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Debriefing of the previous round | 20-30' | Leaderboard and news on the projector view, guiding questions |
| Mini-lecture | 30-45' | The concept needed in the day's rounds |
| Game rounds | 2 × 35-40' | Projected timer; the instructor walks around the teams |
| Wrap-up | 15' | Round report, decision memo assignment |

The guiding questions below also appear in the *Discussion prompts* section of the instructor console, session by session (scaled to the number of rounds in the game), together with questions tied to the events of the round.

The slides for each session, with teacher notes, are in the [English teaching pack](../slide/README.md#teaching-pack-in-english-six-3-hour-sessions).

## Session 1. The founding team and the idea

**Goals**: understand the startup as a temporary organization searching for a repeatable business model; choose sector, co-founders and equity split with awareness.

**Mini-lecture** (40'): startups and small businesses (Blank); the four risks (market, product, team, financing); Wasserman's founding choices: whom to found with, how to split equity, vesting and cliff.

**Activities**
1. *Practice game* (30'): each student plays 3-4 rounds in **Solo vs bots**, **Essential** complexity, to learn the interface. Not graded.
2. *Forming teams* (15') and *founding* (30'): the instructor creates the class game; teams choose name, sector, founder archetypes, equity split and vesting. Suggest reading the sector cards: margin, typical churn, market size and MVP cost differ a lot.
3. *Round 1* (35'): first budget, discovery, MVP development.

**Debriefing**
- What is the riskiest assumption in your idea? How will you test it while spending little?
- Why this team? What would Wasserman say about your equity split (equal or not, and why)?
- How many months of runway do you want to keep?

**Assignment**: decision memo for round 1 (template in [Assessment and rubrics](05-assessment-and-rubrics-en.md)).

---

## Session 2. From problem to MVP

**Goals**: use customer discovery to reduce uncertainty; decide when to launch; read the product-market fit score.

**Mini-lecture** (40'): build-measure-learn (Ries); the MVP as an experiment, not as a smaller product; the Sean Ellis test (at least 40% of users very disappointed without the product); technical debt as a high-interest loan.

**Rounds 2 and 3.** In the game: target customers (niche, broad market, premium), discovery level (none, light, deep), development approach (rush, balanced, quality first), launch, managing technical debt.

**Debriefing**
- Launch now or polish the MVP? What do you lose and what do you gain by waiting a round?
- Compare two teams: does the one that did more discovery have a higher PMF? At what cost in cash?
- The first dilemma: the teams that faced it tell the class.
- Niche or broad market? Can the teams that chose a beachhead describe their customer in one sentence?

**Point of comparison**: in the balancing simulations the *lean* bots (deep discovery, quality-first development, modest spending) reach PMF much more often than the *blitz* bots (early launch, high spending from the start), and when the market reaches the chasm the *blitz* bots without PMF lose much of their value. Ask the teams why.

---

## Session 3. Go-to-market and unit economics

**Goals**: set the price against willingness to pay; choose the channel; read CAC, LTV, churn and payback; recognize the chasm.

**Mini-lecture** (45'): pricing and willingness to pay; pricing models (subscription, freemium, usage-based); channels (performance marketing, brand and content, partnerships, sales-led) and their effects on CAC and churn; LTV/CAC of at least 3 as a rule of thumb; Rogers' adoption curve and Moore's chasm. In the game, market adoption grows round after round (competitors and substitutes plus the teams' customers) and usually passes the early adopters' share (16%) around mid-game: from then on a team needs at least 55 PMF points to avoid the CAC penalty. The mentor warns when the chasm is approaching: that is the right moment to discuss it.

**Rounds 4 and 5** (end of the first year and first half of the second).

**Debriefing**
- Do you have product-market fit? Which numbers prove it (retention, Sean Ellis %, LTV/CAC)?
- Is it time to scale marketing, or would more spending only fill a leaky bucket?
- Several teams in the same sector share the same market: knowing this, how does your strategy change?
- Have the external competitors made a move in your sector (price war, campaign, new version)? How did you respond?
- Coopetition: is a partnership with another team worth more with a team in your sector or with one that has similar customers in another sector?

**Short activity** (15'): each team computes the CAC payback by hand (CAC ÷ (price × gross margin), with the values on the Market page) and compares it with the figure shown on the same page.

## Session 4. People and organization

**Goals**: plan the first hires; use equity as compensation; manage the founders' morale, hours and stress.

**Mini-lecture** (35'): the first ten hires; contractors and employees; stock options and the option pool; the hidden cost of 80-hour weeks.

**Rounds 6 and 7.** In the game: auctions for candidates (other teams can bid on the same profiles; candidates weigh salary, equity and reputation and can counter-offer), morale, burnout.

**Debriefing**
- Are you hiring employees or contractors? Which choice is right at this stage?
- Scarce talent: salary or equity? Which candidates preferred what, and why?
- If you lost a candidate in an auction: what would you have offered differently?

---

## Session 5. Financing growth

**Goals**: compare funding sources; read a term sheet; understand dilution, the option pool shuffle and liquidation preferences; face the dilemma of wealth versus control.

**Mini-lecture** (45'): grants, loans, crowdfunding, angels, VCs; SAFEs and conversion; pre-money and post-money; the option pool shuffle; 1x non-participating liquidation preference; board seats (Feld and Mendelson). *Rich vs King* (Wasserman): founders who give up control tend to build more valuable companies, while those who keep it own a larger share of theirs.

**Rounds 8 and 9.** In the game: pitches to investors (who fund few companies per round, so teams compete for the same money), term sheets with valuation, equity and board seats; rescue rounds with a 2x preference for teams that run out of cash; acquisition offers between teams.

**Debriefing**
- Bootstrapping, angels, accelerator or grants: what does each option cost you beyond money?
- How much of the company do you want to own at the end? Rich or King?
- Look at the cap table: how much of the valuation would actually reach the founders with the current preferences?
- If an investor passed on you because another team had better numbers: what should you have shown?

**Short activity** (20'): one team projects its term sheet; the class computes the founders' post-money stake and compares it with the cap table in the game.

---

## Session 6. Exit and final debriefing

**Goals**: evaluate an acquisition offer; read the waterfall; reflect on the whole journey.

**Round 10** (35'), then the instructor ends the game and projects **Final results & debrief**: podium, leaderboard (founder wealth, company valuation or the balanced criterion), the *Rich or King?* chart, valuation over time, each team's path, achievements unlocked.

**Team presentations** (8' each, plus 4' of questions): strategy, three key decisions, what you would do differently, link to a real case.

**Final debriefing**
- If you received an acquisition offer today, at what price would you sell? Why?
- If there was an acquisition between teams: who gained more, the buyer or the seller?
- Which decision would you take differently, and what did it teach you?
- Where does the game oversimplify reality? (A great prompt for assessing critical thinking.)

**Assignment**: final report and post-mortem within one week.

## Variants

### One-day intensive (6-7 hours)
**Standard** or **Essential** complexity, 6-8 rounds with the **Semesters** timeline. Morning: founding, 3 rounds with 15' mini-lectures. Afternoon: 3-4 rounds, final debriefing and short presentations. Assessment: a single final report.

### Short track for executive and professional courses (4 sessions of 3 hours)
For managers and professionals in evening or weekend programmes. **Standard** complexity, 8 rounds with the **4 quarters, then semesters** timeline. Session 1: founding and rounds 1-2 (themes of sessions 1 and 2). Session 2: rounds 3-4, go-to-market and unit economics. Session 3: rounds 5-6, people and financing. Session 4: rounds 7-8, exit, final debriefing and presentations. Instead of graded assessment: decision memos shared in class and a team post-mortem presented at the last session.

### Two weeks, with remote rounds
Sessions 2-5 alternate with rounds played at home: the instructor shares the game file on Moodle, teams send their decision file by a deadline, and the instructor imports, advances and shares the game again (see the [Instructor guide](03-instructor-guide-en.md)).

### Master's course in finance
**Advanced** complexity: the Funding page shows the exit waterfall simulator (the preference stack and the payout to each shareholder at different exit values). More time for session 5, with an exercise comparing term sheets and finding the exit value below which the founders receive nothing.

### Large class (over 60 students)
Two parallel games with the **same seed** and, if possible, the same sectors: they start from the same market and see the same macroeconomic events, so the leaderboards are comparable and the final debriefing can compare the strategies of the two classes. The games then diverge with the teams' decisions (candidates hired, reactions of the external competitors, events that hit a random sector): that is exactly the interesting comparison.
